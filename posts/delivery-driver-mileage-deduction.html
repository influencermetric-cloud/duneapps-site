Mileage

DoorDash, Uber Eats, Instacart and Grubhub mileage: the 2026 deduction, worked out

The apps send you a mileage number in January or February. It is the smallest your deduction could be, not the deduction. Here is how to work out the real one, and what you need to prove it.

By Nahid SaleemUpdated 15 November 20266 min read

Checked against the IRS standard mileage rates page, Publication 463 and each platform’s own help pages on 15 November 2026.

Key takeaways
  • The sum: January to June business miles × 72.5¢, plus July to December × 76¢, plus business parking and tolls.
  • Uber’s tax summary counts online miles. DoorDash emails an estimate of on-delivery miles. We found no tax mileage report from Instacart or Grubhub.
  • Miles driven while you work count, including between orders. The first drive from home and the last drive home are the ones the IRS may call commuting.
  • You cannot deduct an estimate. Publication 463 wants a written record of the date, the place, the purpose and the miles, kept at or near the time.
  • For 2026 payments a platform only has to send a 1099-NEC at $2,000. The income is taxable either way, and so is the deduction available.

#The sum

The 2026 deduction for a delivery driver using the standard mileage rate is three numbers added together:

PartHow it is worked out
January to JuneBusiness miles driven from 1 January to 30 June × $0.725
July to DecemberBusiness miles driven from 1 July to 31 December × $0.76
Parking and tollsBusiness parking fees and tolls, at what you actually paid

The rate changed on 1 July, which is why the year splits in two. Each trip takes the rate in force on the day you drove it. Applying one rate to the whole year is wrong for half of the year whichever rate you pick, and the 2026 rate page covers what to do if you have already filed that way.

The rate covers fuel, so do not add fuel receipts on top. Parking and tolls are different. Publication 463, on the standard mileage rate: "In addition to using the standard mileage rate, you can deduct any business-related parking fees and tolls."

Delivery mileage deduction, 2026

Use the business miles from your own log, split at 30 June because the IRS rate changed on 1 July. Works the same for DoorDash, Uber Eats, Instacart, Grubhub, or all four together.

A worked example

A driver logs 6,200 business miles from January to June and 5,800 from July to December, and pays $140 in tolls and parking on deliveries.

  • 6,200 × $0.725 = $4,495.00
  • 5,800 × $0.76 = $4,408.00
  • Parking and tolls: $140.00
  • Deduction: $9,043.00

Because a driver is self-employed, that deduction lowers two taxes. Self-employment tax is 12.4% for Social Security plus 2.9% for Medicare, and the IRS applies it to 92.35% of net earnings, so $9,043 less profit is roughly $1,278 less self-employment tax. At a 12% income tax bracket it is about $1,085 less income tax as well. Around $2,360 in total, from a log. The calculator above does the same sum with your numbers, as an estimate: it leaves out the qualified business income deduction and assumes your earnings are under the Social Security wage base.

#What each app's mileage number counts

Every platform's figure stops somewhere short of your real business driving. These are the platforms' own descriptions, read on 15 November 2026:

PlatformWhat it gives youWhat that leaves out
Uber and Uber EatsA tax summary, available by 31 January, with your online miles. Uber says that covers waiting for a trip, the drive to the pickup and the trip itself.Everything you drive with the app off. A single yearly total, with no dates, places or purposes.
DoorDashAn email by 28 February with an estimate of your on-delivery mileage, for Dashers who delivered by car.Driving while you wait for the next offer. DoorDash's own help page says the estimate may not reflect all of your deductible business miles.
InstacartAn estimated mileage for each batch, shown before you accept it.We found no yearly mileage report for tax in Instacart's help pages. A pre-acceptance estimate is a planning number, not a record of the drive.
GrubhubYour 1099, in the driver app and portal from February.Grubhub's driver help on tax forms describes the 1099 and mentions no mileage report. Assume none.

Two things follow. First, if you drive for more than one app, no single platform's number can be right: miles spent waiting for DoorDash while Uber was off are in neither of them. Second, the platform numbers are still worth keeping, because they are records made by somebody else. Next to your own log they show the log is not invented.

#Which miles count

The driving you do while you are working is business driving: to the restaurant or the store, to the customer, and between one job and the next while you are available for offers. Most of a delivery day is that.

The disputed part is the first and last leg of the day. Publication 463 is direct about commuting: "You can't deduct commuting expenses no matter how far your home is from your regular place of work." A delivery driver has no regular place of work, and for that case the rule is that you "can't deduct daily transportation costs between your home and temporary work sites within your metropolitan area."

The exception is a home office. If your home qualifies as your principal place of business, Publication 463 says "you can deduct your daily transportation costs between your home and another work location in the same trade or business." Whether a driver's home qualifies is a test in Publication 587, and it is the question to put to a tax professional if your first and last legs are long.

The practical answer is to log those legs as separate entries with their own start and end. If your home office counts, they are already in the log. If it does not, you leave them out of the total and the rest of the record still stands. A log that blends the drive from home into the first delivery cannot be split later.

#What the log has to show

The IRS will not accept a figure worked out at the end of the year. Publication 463: "You can't deduct amounts that you approximate or estimate." What it accepts is a written record, and for car use it asks for:

  • the date you used the car,
  • your business destination,
  • the business purpose,
  • the mileage for each business use, and the total miles for the year, which is why the odometer reading on 1 January and 31 December matters.

Delivery work has one advantage here, and Publication 463 names it: "the nature of your work, such as making deliveries, provides circumstantial evidence of the use of your car for business purposes. Invoices of deliveries establish when you used the car for business." Your order history in each app is that evidence. Keep a copy of it with the log.

It has to be kept at the time, or near it. The weekly version is fine: "If you maintain a log on a weekly basis that accounts for use during the week, the log is considered a timely kept record." A year written up from memory in March is not that. What the IRS requires in a mileage log goes through each element, and the free mileage log has the columns, as a PDF, a spreadsheet with the 2026 rates built in, and a CSV.

If months are already gone, the rules allow less than most advice suggests and more than you might fear: what to do when you forgot to track your miles covers the incomplete-records rule and sampling.

The higher threshold means a driver who earned $1,500 on one app may get no form from it. The income is still yours to report on Schedule C, and the car expenses go on the same form. A missing 1099 does not shrink the deduction either.

Questions people ask

Does DoorDash track my miles for taxes?

DoorDash emails US Dashers an estimate of their on-delivery mileage, by 28 February. Its own help page says the estimate may not reflect all of your deductible business miles and asks you to keep your own records. It is a useful check on your log, not a replacement for it.

Can I use the online miles on my Uber tax summary as my deduction?

Use them to check your log, not as the log. Uber counts miles driven while you are online: waiting for a trip, on the way to a pickup and during the trip. That total has no dates, places or purposes, which is what the IRS asks a record to show. It also misses business driving you did offline, and it can include driving the IRS treats as commuting.

Can I deduct gas as well as the mileage rate?

No. The standard mileage rate stands in for the whole cost of running the car, fuel included. Business parking fees and tolls are the exception: Publication 463 says you can deduct them in addition to the standard mileage rate.

Is the drive from home to my first pickup deductible?

It depends on whether your home is your principal place of business. If it is, Publication 463 lets you deduct the trip from home to another work location. If it is not and you have no regular place of work, the IRS treats daily trips from home to places inside your metropolitan area as commuting. Log those legs as their own entries, so either answer is supported, and ask a tax professional which applies to you.

Do I need a 1099 to claim the mileage deduction?

No. The deduction depends on the driving and the record, not the form. For payments made in 2026 the reporting threshold for a 1099-NEC rose from $600 to $2,000, so many part-time drivers will not get one. The income still goes on Schedule C, and the car expenses go on the same form.
