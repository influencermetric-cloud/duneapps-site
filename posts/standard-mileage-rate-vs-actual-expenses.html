Standard mileage rate vs actual expenses: which one wins for 2026

One number settles it: what your car costs for every mile you drive, against the IRS rate. Here is how to work that out, a calculator that does it for you, and the rules that stop you changing your mind later.

By Nahid SaleemUpdated 18 November 20268 min read

Checked against the IRS standard mileage rates page, Notice 2026-10, Publication 463 and the draft 2026 Schedule C instructions on 16 November 2026.

Key takeaways
  • The test: divide what the car cost you for the year by every mile you drove. If that beats the IRS rate, actual expenses wins. If not, the standard rate wins.
  • The 2026 rate is 72.5¢ a mile to 30 June and 76¢ from 1 July, so compare against your own average of the two.
  • A modest car driven a lot, the usual delivery or rideshare setup, does better on the standard rate. An expensive car driven fewer miles can do better on actual expenses.
  • Choose with care in the car's first year. Standard first keeps both options open. Actual first, with accelerated depreciation, generally closes the standard rate for that car for good. A leased car stays on whichever method you start with.
  • Business parking, tolls and the business share of car loan interest are deductible under either method. On the 2026 Schedule C the interest has its own line, 16b.

#The one division that decides it

The two methods look like different worlds. One is a flat rate per business mile. The other is a stack of fuel, insurance and repair receipts. Underneath, they compare the same two numbers.

With actual expenses you deduct your car costs multiplied by your business use. Publication 463 works out business use from miles: "You are a contractor and drive your car 20,000 miles during the year: 12,000 miles for business use and 8,000 miles for personal use. You can claim only 60% (12,000 ÷ 20,000) of the cost of operating your car as a business expense."

So actual expenses equals your business miles multiplied by what the car costs for every mile you drive. The standard method equals your business miles multiplied by the IRS rate. The business miles are the same on both sides. The method that wins is the one with the bigger number per mile.

Total car costs for the year ÷ total miles driven, business and personal. If that is above the IRS rate, actual expenses wins. If it is below, the standard rate wins.

For 2026 the IRS rate is two numbers, because it changed on 1 July: 72.5 cents a mile to 30 June and 76 cents from 1 July. Your own rate is somewhere between the two, depending on when you drove. The calculator works it out.

#Two worked examples

A delivery driver with a paid-off sedan

She drives 30,000 miles in 2026, of which 24,000 are for deliveries: 12,000 before 30 June and 12,000 after. The car costs $3,600 in fuel, $1,800 in insurance, $1,400 in repairs and tires and $200 in registration. Say depreciation comes to $2,500 for the year; the real figure would come from Form 4562.

  • Standard rate: 12,000 × $0.725 + 12,000 × $0.76 = $17,820
  • Actual expenses: $9,500 of costs × 80% business use = $7,600
  • Cost per mile: $9,500 ÷ 30,000 = 31.7 cents, less than half the rate

The standard rate wins by $10,220. Fuel is the cost people think of, and fuel was only $3,600 of a $17,820 deduction. The rate pays for a car's whole running cost at a national figure, and a cheap car driven hard costs less per mile than that figure.

A realtor with a leased SUV

He drives 12,000 miles in 2026, of which 9,000 are showing homes and meeting clients, split evenly across the year. The lease costs $9,600, fuel $2,400, insurance $2,400, maintenance $300 and registration $600.

  • Standard rate: 4,500 × $0.725 + 4,500 × $0.76 = $6,682.50
  • Actual expenses: $15,300 of costs × 75% business use = $11,475
  • Cost per mile: $15,300 ÷ 12,000 = $1.275, well above the rate

Actual expenses wins by about $4,800 before one adjustment: a car leased for 30 days or more may carry an inclusion amount that reduces the lease deduction, worked out from a table in Publication 463. And because it is a lease, the choice he makes in the first year holds for the whole lease, which is the next section.

#What each method lets you deduct, line by line

The 2026 Schedule C has a new line for car loan interest. The draft instructions, dated 8 October 2026: "Report the business-use portion of vehicle loan interest on line 16b, regardless of whether you use the business standard mileage rate or the actual costs of operating your vehicle."

The standard rate is not just fuel. Publication 463 is blunt about it: if you use the standard rate, "You can't deduct depreciation, lease payments, maintenance and repairs, gasoline (including gasoline taxes), oil, insurance, or vehicle registration fees." So fuel receipts do not go on top of the rate. Which receipts you actually need covers what to keep under each method.

#The first-year rule, and the other ways to get locked in

This is the part that matters more than any single year's numbers. Publication 463: "If you want to use the standard mileage rate for a car you own, you must choose to use it in the first year the car is available for use in your business. Then, in later years, you can choose to use either the standard mileage rate or actual expenses."

SituationWhat the rules allow
Owned car, standard rate in year oneEither method in later years. A switch to actual expenses uses straight line depreciation over the car's remaining estimated life.
Owned car, actual expenses in year oneNo standard rate later if you claimed MACRS, a section 179 deduction or bonus depreciation on it.
Leased carThe standard rate must be used for the entire lease period. The instructions add that you cannot use actual expenses for a leased vehicle once you have used the standard rate on it.
Five or more cars used at the same timeNo standard rate for any of them. Fleets use actual expenses.
Business use 50% or lessActual expenses still work, but MACRS is out: Publication 463 requires more than 50% business use for it, so depreciation is straight line.

The choice is made on the return itself, by its due date including extensions, and Publication 463 says "You can't revoke the choice." If you started using a car for work in 2026, the return you file in early 2027 is where it gets made.

When the numbers are close in year one, the standard rate is the choice that keeps both doors open. When actual expenses wins by a wide margin, as with the leased SUV above, it can be worth closing the door. Run both before you file, every year the choice is still yours.

#The depreciation inside the rate

The standard rate is not free of depreciation. Part of every mile is treated as wear on the car, and Notice 2026-10 sets that part: "the portion of the business standard mileage rate treated as depreciation is 26 cents per mile for 2022, 28 cents per mile for 2023, 30 cents per mile for 2024, 33 cents per mile for 2025, and 35 cents per mile for 2026." The 1 July announcement raised the rate itself and left every other provision of the notice in effect, so 35 cents applies to the whole of 2026.

Each business mile you claim at the standard rate lowers the car's basis by that amount. 15,000 business miles in 2026 is a $5,250 reduction. You feel it in two places: when you sell or trade in the car, because the gain or loss is worked out from the lower basis, and if you move to actual expenses later, because depreciation starts from what is left.

#The records each method needs

Neither method excuses you from a mileage log. Whichever you use, the IRS asks for your total miles for the year split into business, commuting and other: on Schedule C, Part IV, or on Form 4562 if you are claiming depreciation. Both ask whether your evidence is written.

Questions people ask

Which is better, the standard mileage rate or actual expenses?

Whichever gives the larger figure for your car, and one division tells you. Add up what the car cost for the year (fuel, insurance, repairs, tires, registration, and depreciation or lease payments) and divide by the total miles you drove, business and personal. If the result is more than the IRS rate, 72.5 cents to 30 June 2026 and 76 cents from 1 July, actual expenses gives the bigger deduction. If it is less, the standard rate does.

Can I switch from actual expenses to the standard mileage rate?

Usually not for the same car. Publication 463 says you cannot use the standard rate on a car if you have claimed depreciation on it under MACRS, a section 179 deduction, or the special depreciation allowance. For a leased car, the standard rate must be used for the whole lease if you want it at all. Going the other way, from the standard rate to actual expenses, is allowed in a later year, with straight line depreciation from then on.

Can I deduct my car payments?

Not as payments. Under either method you can deduct the business share of the interest on a car loan, which goes on line 16b of the 2026 Schedule C. The cost of the car itself comes back through depreciation, which is built into the standard rate, or claimed on line 13 under actual expenses. Lease payments are different: under actual expenses the business share goes on line 20a.

Do I need a mileage log if I use actual expenses?

Yes. Actual expenses are deductible only in proportion to business use, and Publication 463 divides them by miles: a car driven 20,000 miles with 12,000 for business can claim 60% of its costs. That percentage comes from a log of business miles and the total miles for the year. Schedule C, Part IV, or Form 4562 when you claim depreciation, asks for both.

What is the depreciation part of the 2026 standard mileage rate?

35 cents a mile, set in IRS Notice 2026-10, and the July rate change did not alter it. Every business mile you claim at the standard rate reduces the car's basis by that amount. It matters when you sell the car, or if you switch to actual expenses later and start depreciating what is left.
